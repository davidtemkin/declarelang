The shares below are my rough estimates from memory of the session. Elapsed time and thinking don't line up perfectly, so I've ranked by both.

## 1. Where the time and thinking went

**1. Reading before writing: about 18%.**
This was declare.md, guide chapters 01, 04, 05 and 06, the to-do app, the tracker, and then about ten more chapters and many `declare-help` lookups. It was large because of how Declare works, not because of the problem: the skill says outright that nothing carries over from what I know. It paid off. Most of what I wrote compiled close to first time, and the tracker gave me Transactions almost whole. If anything I under-read: chapter 03 (constraints) would likely have spared me most of the "not a nameable path" errors.

**2. The charts (PeriodChart and RankRow): about 15%.**
Most of this was the problem itself. The brief asks a lot of every chart: grouped bars, lines, a dashed comparison series, shaded partial periods, an axis starting at zero, a pointer card, keyboard travel across both periods and series, a sentence for each point, every mark opening its records, drag to select a range, and animation between datasets. On top of that I was unsure how Declare *should* express a chart, because there's no chart primitive and I skipped marketmap. The design I settled on — one `draw` for the plot, with transparent `Control`s laid over it, one per period, in a `focusScope = stop` group — came out of thinking, not pushback. Where the platform did push back: DECLARE7001 on `seriesValue(p, …)`, the `onClick(e)` signature refusal, and the `valueOf`/`blend` name clashes. Together that was perhaps a fifth of the chart time.

**3. Planning the app as a whole: about 12%.**
This covered the URL grammar (`shows` matches exactly, so I settled on "screen in `location`, filter on the App, `returnTo` for Back"), splitting the data into Books and Figures, fetching the whole list versus pages (I measured 1.1 MB in 24 ms), the comparison-period semantics, and which figure each drill-down maps to. This was mostly the problem plus reading the API. It went quickly once I had the four-part plan the skill asks for (data, state, classes, changes).

**4. Checking — the nine assert scripts and the screenshots: about 12%.**
I iterated on most scripts two or three times. About half of the failures were in my scripts: a locator matching two elements, a role I guessed wrong for menu items, the 1024-wide default window, a no-break space breaking a regex, a run that wasn't idempotent. The other half found real bugs: Tab never reaching a text field's key handler, the debounced slider stalling on repeated keys, no detail pane at medium widths. The tools themselves served me well; the friction was mostly that I didn't know their defaults.

**5. Getting layout right: about 10%.**
Mostly platform pushback, and the pushback was correct. In order: RankRow overflowing at 1024 wide, then a refused fix (a width set by both the layout and the view itself), panels flexing along a column whose axis switches with width (they collapsed to 0 height), a stacked `Field` not filling its column, a scroller sized by its own content, a cell editor overflowing its row, then the phone's 44×44 targets and the slim sidebar. Each fix was small once I'd read the message. They added up because there were many, and because I had carried CSS instincts over: "flex works along either axis", "a field fills its column".

**6. The DatePicker error inside library code: about 8%.**
One obstacle, not a pattern. The error pointed at library code I hadn't written, and my bisecting kept being fooled: cutting code to isolate it introduced type errors, and those stopped the analysis before it reached this check. In the end I worked around it rather than understood it. All of this was the platform pushing back in a way I couldn't read.

**7. The editor (one form for expense, sale and refund): about 8%.**
The problem was moderately complex (split parts, product lines, refundable quantities, creating a vendor and then saving). How Declare expresses it was clear, thanks to `Form`: the `refusal` override, `validate()`, field names matching the service's paths. Pushback was small: `rowIndex` inside nested controls, paths through `Field`, `Segmented` having no `name`. It passed its flows (2 and 3) on the first run, which surprised me.

**8. The filters (bar, panels, chips, amount slider): about 7%.**
The `AmountRange` slider is covered in section 2. The rest was straightforward. The phone version (a "Filters" sheet) came late, prompted by a screenshot and the finger-size findings.

**9. Transactions — table, in-place editing, detail, bulk bar: about 6%.**
Small for its importance, because Table did most of it. The hard part was in-place editing — Tab, and both lines making way for the editor — which landed in checking.

**10. Polish — copy, lint rewrites, formatting: about 4%.**
Prepositions in the sentences, "unchanged" instead of "down 0%", the no-break spaces, nested ternaries rewritten as named facts and `State`s.

## 2. Writing my own classes and controls

These are the custom pieces and how each went.

**PeriodChart (time chart).**
It took two real attempts:
- On the first compile it hit `valueOf` and `blend`, which are names the platform already owns, then the `onClick(e)` signature refusal, then DECLARE7001 on passing a local point into a method.
- After those fixes it worked the first time I looked at it: the bars, lines, card and focus all rendered correctly in the first screenshots.

Easy: `draw` with Canvas-style calls, and getting focus, roles, Enter and Space for free by making each period a `Control`. Hard: knowing which values a method may be handed (the 7001 rule), animating arrays (I used an Animator on a 0–1 `mix` plus a blend constraint), and getting the click position inside `onClick` (I used `app.pointerX` minus `rootOrigin()`, which I'm unsure is sanctioned). Drag-to-select I wrote but never drove, so I'm unsure it works. What would have made it faster: a chart example built this way in the guide, or a statement that the 7001 rule applies to any argument that isn't a named path.

**RankRow (ranked horizontal bar).** Three tries, all about layout: a fixed name width overflowed, a width set by both view and layout was refused, then `flexes` with `minWidth` worked. Otherwise easy: a Spring on the bar's width, colour by category, a sentence as its accessibility label.

**AmountRange and AmountHandle (two-handled amount slider).**
About three tries:
- First, `onPointerDown(e)` with `super.onPointerDown(e)` was refused; I changed it to the Knob example's no-argument form using `app.pointerX`.
- Second, repeated Page Up stalled, because the handle showed the debounced filter value once each edit ended. I added pending `_lo` and `_hi` values.
- Third, a 44-px hit area around the 22-px knob for phones.

Easy: `ValueControl` with `beginEdit`/`edit`/`endEdit` meant the handle followed the hand regardless of the filter. The slider role, value text and increment methods gave correct screen-reader semantics, and the histogram was a short `draw`. Hard: understanding that the edit ends after each key press and the control then shows its value again. That is documented; I just didn't connect it to the debounce. What would have helped: a range slider in the library, or a note in chapter 08 about debouncing.

**CellField and CellChoice (in-place cell editors).**
Two tries:
- First: a missing `commit` (DECLARE2000, clear).
- Second: Tab never reached `onKeyDown`, which I only discovered because a test failed with no error. I moved Tab handling to the screen's `Keys` member, with a somewhat ugly cast of `Focus.getFocus()`.

The Escape and Return paths and the Combobox reuse were easy. I'd have needed to know how TextInput handles Tab.

**TxnRow (TableRow subclass with a phone variant and day headings).** It worked nearly first time: TableRow, `virtualize`, the heading belonging to the row that starts a day, `exists` for the editors. One layout fix while editing.

**Editor (Form subclass).** It worked on its first flow run after the structural fixes. `Form` was the best surprise of the build.

**Small parts.** KpiCard, Delta, Sparkline, CategoryChip, FilterChip, FilterButton, Panel with load and fail states, the Watch "slow" node, the Relax node, NavItem, PhoneTab, ProductTile, SaleLineRow, PartRow and RefundLineRow were all easy: one try each, apart from the `rowIndex` warning and the lint-driven rewrite of nested ternaries into States.

**Overall.** Making something a `Control` or `ValueControl` gave me keyboard, focus and accessibility nearly for free, which is the big win. The costs were platform rules I met only as errors (7001, signature compatibility, reserved names) and two input-routing questions (Tab in a field, the click position) that I settled by experiment.

## 3. Compared with React and its ecosystem (the one I know best)

**What I would have reached for:**
- **Charts:** Recharts, Nivo, or Visx.
  - *Fit:* grouped bars plus a line plus a dashed comparison series, axes, tooltips — all off the shelf in Recharts, in a fraction of my time.
  - *Where it wouldn't fit:* the brief's accessibility: one Tab stop per chart, arrows across periods *and* series, and a sentence per point. Recharts' keyboard support is minimal, so I'd be layering my own focus management over SVG, or dropping to Visx and building interaction myself. Opening the exact records behind a mark is easy with click handlers; for keyboard it isn't. Shaded partial periods and drag-to-select need custom layers. Animating between datasets is built in (an advantage).
  - *Net:* faster to something that looks finished; roughly equal or worse effort to meet the accessibility clauses. In Declare, getting a chart on screen cost more, but the accessible version cost less.
- **Range slider:** Radix Slider or React Aria's range slider.
  - *Fit:* very good. Two thumbs, keyboard, ARIA names, no crossing, Page Up and Page Down, all out of the box.
  - *Where it wouldn't fit:* the non-linear scale on the histogram's bucket edges, which I'd do by mapping to an index scale, and the histogram drawn above the track, which is custom either way.
  - *Net:* clearly less effort than my AmountRange. Declare's lack of a range slider is a real gap here.
- **Table:** TanStack Table with TanStack Virtual, or AG Grid.
  - *Fit:* AG Grid gives cell editing with Tab, Enter and Escape, row selection with shift-ranges, sorting and virtualization, but its look and keyboard model are its own. The two-line description cell, chips, highlighted search text, and phone rows grouped by day would mean a lot of custom cell renderers fighting its styling. TanStack is headless: I'd write all the selection and keyboard behaviour that Declare's `Table` gave me, plus virtualization wiring.
  - *Net:* Declare's `Table` was the better fit for this brief. Less code than TanStack, and less fighting than AG Grid, except for in-place editing, where AG Grid's built-in Tab behaviour would have saved me the workaround.
- **Forms:** React Hook Form with Zod.
  - *Fit:* good for validation and focusing the first error. Mapping the server's field-addressed refusals (`parts[1].amount`) onto fields needs a small adapter, as Declare's `refusal` override did. Comparable effort; Declare's `Form` was slightly more turnkey.
- **Overlays, menus, combobox, toast:** Radix or React Aria, plus Sonner.
  - *Fit:* comparable to Declare's library, which matched them for this brief. A combobox with a "Create “Printworks”" item is a little more work in Radix; Declare's was easy, because its items are just data.
- **Data fetching:** TanStack Query.
  - *Fit:* gives caching, keeping the previous data while loading, and cancelling stale requests. Comparable to Declare's `DataSource` but more setup, and the query keys must be kept consistent by hand. Declare's derived `url` was simpler and less error-prone.

**Overall, I'm moderately confident** that a React build would get to a good-looking first version faster, mainly because of charts and the range slider. Meeting the brief's keyboard, accessibility and reconciliation clauses would take about as much effort in either. Declare's built-ins (Table, Form, DataSource, focus scopes, accessibility warnings, layout findings) covered more of those exact clauses out of the box. Where I'd have built my own anyway — the accessible chart and the histogram slider — building it in Declare was about as much work as it would have been in React with Visx and React Aria, and possibly less for the accessibility half.

I'm unsure on two points. I haven't built this exact app in React, so these are judgements, not measurements. And Declare's two rough edges here — the 7001 rule and the DatePicker issue — have no real equivalent in React, so they shift the comparison by a margin I can't size precisely.
