## 1. Where the time and thinking went

These shares are rough, from memory. They cover both thinking and the work that came out of it.

1. **Learning Declare: about 25%.** This was almost entirely not knowing how Declare expresses things. I read declare.md, the four guide chapters and the to-do app whole, then seven more chapters and about forty `declare-help` lookups. Then I wrote a scratch program to test four risks (a recursive schema, coordinates under `scale`, a text field inside a scaled view, a PUT that gets a 404 back). It was expensive, but it paid back: the first real compile needed about ten fixes, not fifty. The scratch program was the best value per minute of the whole build.

2. **Checking, and debugging what the checks found: about 18%.** This was mixed.
   - About half was learning the test tools: reading state from a script, the default window being narrower than my `minWidth`, and `clickAt` not taking modifiers, which sent me into `verify-behave.mjs`.
   - About a third was the double-click/`onBlur` focus problem, which was a platform behaviour I couldn't see into.
   - The rest was genuine app bugs the scripts caught: title focus, and the "Opening…" overlay showing while closing.

3. **Canvas interaction: about 15%.** This covers the `Gesture` node, the handles, hit testing, snapping, resize maths, lines, the radius handle, and entering groups by double-click. It was hard because of the problem itself. Declare added almost nothing to the difficulty. The one Declare fact that mattered was the coordinate system under `scale`, and the scratch test had already settled it. Most of this went into `shapes.ts` as plain TypeScript.

4. **Saving to the service: about 12%.** Hard because of the problem: conflict semantics, deck-level 409s, telling my own in-flight write apart from another window's change, and what the status line should say during a conflict. The Declare side (one `DataSource` per kind of request, each built from a `job` attribute, with `fetch()` settling first) took perhaps a fifth of that. The rest was deciding to compare the document with what the service had accepted, rather than track dirty flags. That decision is what made undo and retries free.

5. **Planning the app as a whole: about 8%.** Deciding where state lives, one `SlideView` at every scale, `visible` versus `exists` for the editor, `Shortcuts` versus `Keys`, and recursive `classFor` versus flattening groups. The skill's structure ("records in datasets, the person's place on the App") made this faster than it would otherwise have been.

6. **The inspector and its controls: about 8%.** Partly the problem: mixed values, and a panel that keeps its shape. Partly Declare: `NumberField` and `HexField` built on `TextInput`, `admit` and `commitOn`, and the accessibility warnings.

7. **Commands, menus and undo: about 7%.** The menus were nearly free, because the `MenuBar` records are the shortcut table. Undo was a designed piece, because the platform has none.

8. **Compiler refusals: about 4%.** Cheap. Every message named its fix. The `bounds` collision was the only one that took real thought.

9. **Polish and the secondary screens: about 3–5%.** The home grid, empty state, skeletons, the presenter, and the editor growing out of its card. These were cheap because springs, `TweenLayout` and the overlays did the work.

## 2. Building the custom pieces

**Small controls and visuals**
- `Glyph` (about 45 drawn icons), `Mark`/`Wordmark`, and the empty-home illustration.
- `IconButton`, `ToolChip`, `Swatch`, `Row`, `Heading`, `MixedNote`, `SaveStatus`.
- `DeckCard`, `NewCard`, `SkeletonCard`.

These were easy, and nearly all worked first time. Extending `Control` gave me focus, Space and Enter, and disabled-aware hover and pressed states for free. A `State` on `on` was a clean way to show "selected". `draw()` made icons trivial, and a theme token flowed into them without wiring. There were three second tries:
- **`DeckCard`** was restructured after DECLARE4020 warned about a button inside a button.
- **`IconButton`** gained `accessibilityPressed` after DECLARE4022.
- **`ColorWell`** got a separate look for disabled after the first screenshot.

**Value-entry controls**
- **`HexField` and `NumberField`, both extending `TextInput`.** This mostly went well: `admit` filters keystrokes, and `commitOn = "end"` gives one undo step per edit. There were two snags.
  - DECLARE4031 told me my `onKeyDown` had replaced the Control's own key handling, so I added `super.onKeyDown(e)`.
  - My `deliver(v)` method, which the use site overrides, is close to a pattern the skill warns against. I'm not sure what the idiomatic shape for "a number that can also be Mixed" is.
- **`ColorPicker` (a `Popover` subclass) with a `Swatch` grid.** Easy, first try. Its one limit is that a popover can only open above or below.

**The slide renderer**
- **`SlideView` with `RectView`, `EllipseView`, `LineView`, `TextView` and `GroupView`.** Mostly easy, and recursive `classFor` worked first time.
- **`TextView`** was the hard one and took three rounds:
  1. Laying the text field exactly over the visible text.
  2. Height reporting that wrote to presentations just because they were viewed, which I then restricted to text being worked on.
  3. Ending edits on loss of focus, which I removed.

  I still haven't verified that the field and the text line up exactly for every font.

**Editor machinery**
- **`Grip` and `SelectionChrome`.** Easy as constraints. One pre-emptive rework: I had planned a use site that redeclared the `knob` child. I knew from chapter 17 that this would be refused, so it became a `warm` attribute.
- **`Gesture`, a `Node` with no view.** It worked first time. It's simple because the hard maths sits in `shapes.ts`.
- **`SlideRail`, `RailLayout` and `SlideThumb`.** The glide when slides reorder was free (`TweenLayout`). Dragging was the hard part: I computed the drop slot by hand from `rootOrigin()`, `scrollY` and a fixed spacing. It needed one rename (`key` is reserved). I'd expect bugs when the list is scrolled.
- **`Canvas`, `Inspector`, `TopBar`, `ConflictBanner`, `Presenter`.** Mostly layout, and mostly right first time. `Presenter`'s idle timer was the one bug: the presenter is built at boot and only hidden, so its timer fired before anyone presented.

**What would have made it faster**
- A sentence saying what `e.x` means under `scale`.
- A list of `View`'s own member names, so I'd avoid `bounds` and `key`.
- A way to see why focus moved.
- Library pieces for a colour well, a number field that can show Mixed, a sortable list, and undo.

## 3. Compared with React and its ecosystem

**What I would have reached for prebuilt:**
- **Menus, menu bar, dialogs, popovers, toasts:** Radix or shadcn. That's roughly equal to what Declare ships; Declare's were less wiring, because `MenuBar` doubles as the shortcut table.
- **Colour picker:** react-colorful. A better fit than mine, and faster. I'd still have needed a custom wrapper for "None", "Mixed" and the brand palette.
- **Slide-list reordering:** dnd-kit's sortable list. A clearly better fit than my hand-made version: auto-scroll, keyboard reordering and accessibility announcements come with it. This is where React would have beaten my result.
- **The editor growing out of its card:** Framer Motion's shared-layout animation. Comparable effort; Declare's single spring felt simpler.
- **Undo:** Immer patches, or zundo with Zustand. Easier than hand-written snapshots, though the snapshot approach is equally simple.
- **Selection handles and resizing:** Konva's `Transformer` (react-konva) or react-moveable. Partially fitting.

**Where a prebuilt one would not have fit:**
- **The transformer.** The brief's exact rules — Shift *inverts* keep-proportions, the corner radius doesn't stretch on resize, a text box changes width only, a line has a handle at each end, a radius handle, double-click to go into a group, and snapping to other things' edges — would mean heavy customisation or fighting the library. Konva also owns its own scene model, so I'd keep translating between it and the service's JSON.
- **Whole-editor kits (tldraw, Excalidraw, Fabric.js).** They'd give a lot quickly, but bring their own data model, look and keyboard map. The brief's own format, three exact save sentences, and conflict flow would make adopting one a large adaptation project. I'd likely abandon it.
- **Editing text in place** is notoriously awkward in React too (contentEditable or a positioned textarea). I'd have built it myself anyway, with similar effort. In Declare, a `TextInput` that simply scales with the slide was a pleasant surprise.
- **The inspector's mixed-value states and fixed shape**, "Slide N" labels, the save status and the conflict banner are custom in any framework.

**Effort and result, if built by hand in React:**
- **Thumbnails:** a CSS-scaled version of the same component is just as easy in React. But layout doesn't account for the transform there, so I'd be sizing wrapper boxes by hand. Declare's "every reader agrees on scale" was nicer.
- **Canvas maths:** identical either way, because it's TypeScript either way.
- **Saving:** a React Query mutation queue, or a custom hook. Similar thinking; probably more effect and race-condition wiring than Declare's `DataSource`s, whose `fetch()` settles first.
- **Overall:** my guess is total effort would be similar. React wins on ecosystem pieces (sortable list, colour picker, undo). Declare wins on overlays, motion and derived state, with no glue code.

I'd expect a React version to be better at slide reordering and colour picking, and about the same or slightly worse at motion and keeping every view in step. I'm unsure on that last point, because I didn't build the React version.
